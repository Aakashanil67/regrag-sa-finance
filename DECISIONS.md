# Engineering decisions log

Why the code looks the way it does, written so a reviewer can get through it in ten minutes. Most entries record a mistake or a measurement that changed my mind. Numbers come from the files under `reports/`, and I name the file where it matters.

## Corpus

SARB, NCR and FSCA reject bare `curl` and `requests` calls with a 200 that is really an HTML rejection page, not a 403. It looks like success until you check the content type. A realistic `User-Agent` and a same-site `Referer` passed on all three, with no cookies. `scripts/fetch_corpus.py` sends both on every request. I confirmed it by fetching one file in a browser first, so I knew the URL was fine and the headers were the problem.

Corpus quality beat corpus currency when I picked documents. Two 2004 Basel II circulars are in because they are substantive text, and the newer candidates I tried first turned out to be thin index pages or badly scanned. I later found both circulars are withdrawn under SARB Circular C1/2026, which deems earlier Banks Act circulars withdrawn unless that year's Circular 1 confirms them. They are marked `withdrawn` in the manifest, with C1/2026 recorded as the evidence, and any answer citing one carries a notice generated by code.

That check was part of a live-source audit that found the corpus had drifted from its stated authority in five ways. The OTC derivatives conduct standard is still a 2018 consultation draft. The PwC IFRS 9 guide predates the 2014 standard its metadata implied, so I replaced it with the IFRS Foundation's issued text, dated as a historical snapshot. Some 2004 issuers were credited to a Prudential Authority that did not exist until 2018. Directives 8/2023 and 10/2021 have been superseded by 8/2025 and 4/2023. The manifest schema recorded categories but nothing checked they were still true.

Later I added the FSR Act, the Banks Act, the bank regulations, the credit regulations and Conduct Standard 3 of 2020. The last is a scanned PDF and needed OCR (see below).

Manifest titles were paraphrases at one point, not the documents' real titles. I wrote "Debt Counsellor Contact Information Requirements" for an NCR guideline whose cover page actually says "004/2025 SEPTEMBER 2025", and a question asking about Guideline 004/2025 refused because that string appeared nowhere in what the model saw. I re-read the cover page of every document, not only the four I had already caught.

**OCR.** `reports/ocr_check.md` compares Tesseract 5 at 300 dpi against the page image for 10 pages of Conduct Standard 3 of 2020. It found 47 word errors in 981 words, about 4.8%. The errors are mostly merged words, "I" for a lowercase "l", and mangled list markers. The legal wording stays searchable, but quotes and paragraph letters from that document need checking against the PDF, and the README says so.

## Chunking and embeddings

**The v1.1 embedder was silently truncating most of what it stored.** v1.1 cut chunks at 800 tokens, counted by a generic tokenizer, and the MiniLM embedder reads only 256 wordpieces. 614 of the 795 chunks were truncated, so about three quarters of every long chunk was never embedded. Retrieval still looked fine, because the first 256 wordpieces of a legal paragraph usually carry its topic. It cost me on questions whose answer sat late in a chunk. I found it by measuring truncation directly, not through a failing test.

Two fixes were compared on the 40 dev questions. The `wordpiece` store sizes chunks with the embedder's own tokenizer, giving 6152 chunks, none truncated. The `bge` store swaps in a bge embedder, giving 3238 chunks, none truncated. The `baseline` row is the v1.1 chunking over the current corpus: 1606 of 1857 chunks truncated.

Fixing truncation alone moved task outcome (answerable questions answered plus unanswerable ones refused) from 36 to 38 of 40 and put an evidence page in the top 5 for 34 of 34 answerable questions, up from 32. The all-documents hit rate did not move, 28 of 34 either way. bge did worse than wordpiece on every outcome count: 36 of 40, 26 of 34 all-documents hits.

Two results went the wrong way and I am leaving them in. Without reranking, wordpiece has a lower MRR than the truncated baseline (0.541 against 0.614), and with reranking it is lower again (0.698 against 0.841). I did not chase why. And on multi-document questions with reranking, all-documents hits fell from 5 of 8 to 3 of 8. I selected wordpiece anyway because the rule I had written before the runs put truncation-free configs first, and the answer counts favoured it. That rule counts task outcomes, not MRR, and I would not claim wordpiece retrieves better in every sense. It answered more questions correctly, and that is all the data supports.

**Chunk and page ids include the page.** Chroma ids are `sha256(doc_id:page_start:text)`, not text alone. Legislation repeats identical passages on different pages, and a text-only hash collapsed them into one vector, keeping whichever page's citation was embedded first. The same collapse hid a chunker bug: a stale overlap tail leaking out as its own mini-chunk, producing 24 duplicate groups among 1077 raw chunks. I fixed the reseeding in `flush()` and added a regression test that I confirmed fails on the old code.

**Heading detection uses numbering or relative font size, never one alone.** Numbered list items are not headings, and some documents use a single font size throughout. I accepted some false positives instead of building a layout model for 19 PDFs. It failed twice on dates that looked like headings, first a bare "08 July 2020" and then "28 February 2005." with its full stop. Each time the date text was dropped from the chunk and a question about it refused. Widening the regex fixed the second and I added tests on the real PDFs, since a unit test on the regex alone had already let one through.

## Retrieval

**Exact cosine, not approximate search.** Two back-to-back runs of the same benchmark on the same frozen pipeline scored 93% and 90% with no code change. Chroma's local HNSW index rebuilt its graph on every process start with a thread pool sized to the CPU count, so insertion order and therefore candidate sets varied. Raising `hnsw:search_ef` did nothing, because `collection.modify()` updates the collection metadata, not the running segment. I replaced `.query()` with exact similarity computed in-process, checked it bit-identical across 6 process launches, and kept it because at this scale it costs milliseconds per query.

**BM25 and hybrid were benchmarked, not adopted.** `reports/retrieval_bench.md` covers four strategies (semantic, named-document balanced, BM25, and BM25 plus semantic fused by reciprocal rank) over three stores, with and without the cross-encoder. On the truncated baseline, BM25 alone found an evidence page for 32 of 34 questions with no reranking at 13 ms, against 29 of 34 for semantic search at 262 ms. That looked promising. On the wordpiece store BM25 fell to 28 of 34, and hybrid never beat the wordpiece semantic-plus-rerank pair on all-documents hits (26 of 34 against 28). Hybrid was not selected. In the end-to-end dev run, hybrid answered 30 of 34 and wordpiece 32 of 34.

**Reranking.** In v1.1 I swept chunk size and reranking together (300, 500 and 800 tokens, rerank on and off). 800 with reranking won, 95% and MRR 0.808 on a 20-question set, and I moved the default. I later had to correct how I reported it. 85% to 95% is 17 of 20 against 19 of 20, two questions, with Wilson intervals of 64 to 95% and 76 to 99%. A paired re-analysis of the RAGAS comparison showed the same thing: reranking pulled six refused questions into answers and pushed one back, so the two means were computed on different item sets. On the 33 items both runs scored, faithfulness moved by 0.023 with 9 items improved and 9 worse. Only context precision survived (+0.109, 14 improved against 5, p = 0.06). What reranking bought was coverage, five net refusals turned into answers, and I now say that in the README instead of listing four green arrows.

Reranking costs time. p50 latency rose from about 260 ms to about 2.4 s on the baseline store and from about 650 ms to 1.6 s on wordpiece.

**Multi-document questions stay hard.** A question that needs one chunk from each of two named documents embeds as a single query, which pulls toward whichever document's vocabulary it resembles. Named-document retrieval uses the manifest to balance candidates per document. In the benchmark it changed nothing on multi-document hits: the same 2 of 8 without reranking and 3 of 8 with, on the wordpiece store, as plain semantic search. I left it in as an option and did not make it the default.

## Answering and citations

**Every non-empty answer line must end in a citation to a retrieved page, or the response is refused.** `validate_generated_answer` in `rag.py` is the single gate, and it returns a structured reason (`no_context`, `model_refusal`, `malformed_refusal`, `missing_citation`, `uncited_line`, `unverified_citation`) so the harness and dashboard can say which rule fired. Before v1.1 `agent.py` had its own drifting copy of that logic. Now nothing does.

This check is structural, not semantic. A cited page can exist and still not support the claim. The README used to say "every factual claim is citation checked", which implied more than the mechanism does. Support is a separate question, answered offline by the judges and by human review, and never guaranteed on a live request.

Citations are checked against what was retrieved, not trusted because the model wrote them. The first live run flagged 7 of 9 correct citations as unverified. My `covered_pages` map was overwritten per chunk, not unioned per document, and every mock in the test suite used one chunk per document. A live run with four chunks from one document caught it. Another live run caught the model copying my debugging index ("[1, p.2]") into a citation, so I removed the index from the prompt.

**Refusal is an exact, greppable sentence.** The harness and CI both need to detect a refusal without parsing free text, so a case-insensitive substring match is crude and deterministic.

**Citation repair.** One class of failure kept turning good answers into refusals: correct content, citation format slightly off. v1.1's rc1 refused a right answer because the model wrote `[fsca_rdr_2014, p.11-12, 1.4.1]` and the regex wanted the bracket closed after the page. I loosened the regex and bumped `CITATION_CONTRACT_VERSION`. After that I added a single retry, made only when the sole fault is the citation format. It ran twice across all eight evaluation runs. In one case (bge on dev, item d02) the repaired answer still had an uncited line and the answer was refused. In the other (test, item t11) it was accepted and served. Two cases is too few to say the retry earns its complexity. It stays because the repaired text goes through the same gate, so it cannot serve an unverified citation.

**Source notices are code, not model output.** Type, year, issuer and title of each context block are printed unbracketed so the model cannot copy them into a citation. A fixed notice is generated whenever a cited source is third-party commentary or a withdrawn or superseded circular. It lives in `RAGResult.source_notices`, never appended to `answer`, so the cache key, citation extraction and scoring keep reading exactly what the model wrote. The currency rule is scoped by document type, not a year cutoff: the National Credit Act is from 2005 and is still the governing statute.

Checking this against the live model surfaced a cost I had not expected. Golden item `g45` started refusing, because with only a brochure in front of it the model now declined to attribute a claim to "the Act itself" when the Act's own chunk had been ranked 6th by the reranker and dropped from the top 5. The notice did not cause the gap. It stopped the model answering around it. The reranker's behaviour on that kind of comparison question remains an open finding.

**Models and cost.** v1.1 generated with Claude Haiku 4.5 (list price $1.00 in, $5.00 out per million tokens). v1.2 generates with GPT-5.6 Luna ($0.20 in, $1.20 out, both from `src/config.py`), roughly a fifth of the input price and a quarter of the output price. The whole test run of 60 questions cost $0.0298 through the final pipeline, about $0.0005 per question. All eight evaluation runs together (five dev, three test) cost $0.2345.

I re-ran the v1.1 pipeline on Luna as the `baseline` config, so before and after use the same generator. Comparing v1.2-on-Luna to v1.1-on-Haiku would have measured two changes at once, and I made that same mistake with the RAGAS means earlier. The judges are local Ollama models (Qwen 2.5 7B and Llama 3.1 8B). They cost nothing, they come from different families than the generator and from each other, and they cannot be swayed by it.

**Closed-book baseline.** GPT-5.6 Luna with no retrieved context answered 28 of 34 answerable dev questions and refused 1 of 6 unanswerable ones, for 29 of 40 overall. On the test set it answered 42 of 48 and refused 0 of 12, for 42 of 60. It is fluent without the documents, and it almost never declines. Retrieval is what stops it answering things the corpus cannot support. Answering, on its own, is not the gap.

## Evaluation

**Questions carry quoted evidence.** v1.1 questions had a reference answer and a page. v1.2 questions carry the quoted passage as well, so a human can check a question in a minute and a judge can grade against text, not a paraphrase. Dev questions were written by hand for practitioners (40 of them: 34 answerable, 6 unanswerable) and the unanswerable ones are anchored in banking, not weather, so refusing them is a real test of grounding.

**The selection rule was written before the runs.** Compare task outcome on the 40 dev questions for `wordpiece`, `bge`, `hybrid` and `baseline`, and pick the first config in that order whose count is within one of the best. `reports/dev_selection.md` records it. Wordpiece scored 38 of 40, the others 36. I did not retune anything after seeing the results.

**The test set was sealed and run once.** 60 questions (48 answerable, 12 unanswerable) were fixed and hashed before I tuned anything against them. I froze the pipeline in a commit and ran it once. The final result, with 95% Wilson intervals:

| | final | baseline (v1.1 on Luna) | closed book |
|---|---|---|---|
| answered | 37/48 (77%) | 37/48 (77%) | 42/48 (88%) |
| refused unanswerable | 11/12 (92%) | 11/12 (92%) | 0/12 (0%) |
| correct outcome | 48/60 (80%, 68 to 88%) | 48/60 (80%) | 42/60 (70%, 57 to 80%) |
| evidence page in top k | 40/48 (83%) | 37/48 (77%) | 0/48 |
| every evidence document in top k | 32/48 (67%) | 29/48 (60%) | 0/48 |

The outcome count did not move. The new pipeline retrieves better, three more questions with an evidence page and three more with every evidence document, and that did not become more answers. The intervals overlap heavily and I make no claim of a difference. Citations were valid throughout: 82 of 82 model citations pointed at a page the model had been shown, no unverified citation was served, and the pipeline's 22 test refusals were all model refusals.

**A two-label rubric judge replaced RAGAS.** Answer relevancy measured nothing a user needed, and the RAGAS judge disagreed with manual inspection on three items I checked by hand. `g16` scored 0.00 on relevancy for a correct answer, apparently because it carried a hedge. `g41` scored 0.00 on context precision though both retrieved chunks were exactly right. `g40` scored 0.26 on faithfulness for a figure I confirmed verbatim against the PDF. Earlier, 11 of 45 items scored 0.00 on every metric because refusals were fed to metrics built for answers, a bug in my harness that made the first aggregate look worse than it was (0.771/0.615/0.538/0.778 against 0.791/0.851/0.673/0.912 once refusals were counted separately). I dropped the framework. Every answer is now graded `correct`, `partial` or `incorrect` against the reference, and RAG answers are also graded `supported`, `partial` or `unsupported` against the retrieved context. Refusals are counted from the run, not graded.

**Two local judges disagree, and I report that.** On the wordpiece dev run Qwen gave 16 correct, 14 partial, 2 incorrect. Llama gave 24 correct, 1 partial, 7 incorrect. Cohen's kappa on the 32 items both graded was 0.320 (raw agreement 0.594), and 0.512 on the closed-book run. They never split on the extremes; the disagreement is all in Qwen's `partial`. Qwen marks down for extra material and Llama marks down for a wrong specific. Both called all 32 RAG answers `supported`, so the support axis has nothing to compare. A kappa of 0.32 means I treat these as a cheap second opinion and not ground truth, which is why 20 answers go to a human.

**Human review.** `reports/review_packet_v1.2.md` and `reports/review_v1.2.csv` hold 20 answers and a blank form using the same rubric. At the time of writing the form is unfilled, so I report no human-versus-judge kappa. I will add it when the reviewer returns the form, and I have written no number in its place.

**Earlier evaluation lessons that still apply.** A mean over a changing item set measures two things at once, so compare on the paired items. The faithfulness floor in CI was set after seeing the data (0.5 first, since a 0.7 guess would have failed the recorded subset itself), then went stale when the config changed and had to be raised to 0.65, since a floor a third below its mean is not a gate. Reusing a tuned development set as release evidence makes every later number partly a measurement of what was tuned against, so dev and test are separate files and the test hash is pinned. The v1.1 holdout, its CI fixture and three run artifacts are kept as `historical_reused` evidence, useful as observations, not as current release evidence.

## Privacy and caching

Query and answer text are not logged by default. `LOG_RAW_CONTENT` is false, and `src/obslog.py` stores only metrics: timings, cost, refusal reason, citation counts, model, injection flag and cache hit. A tool for regulatory questions is exactly where someone pastes a real account number without thinking. A local user who sets `LOG_HASH_KEY` gets an HMAC-SHA256 fingerprint of the question, which I call pseudonymous because anyone holding the key can reverse it. `LOG_RETENTION_DAYS` (30) bounds how long any row survives.

The response cache is exact-match on normalised question text, opt-in, and expires. A semantic cache would hit more often but risks serving a cached answer to a subtly different question, and this tool's whole premise is citation accuracy. The cache key also includes a fingerprint of the models, the collection, the chunk settings and a hash of the system prompt. It was originally keyed on question text alone, so the config change that took chunk size to 800 never invalidated it, and the running API served pre-change answers while my reports described the new configuration. Every measurement path happened to route around the cache, which is why it went unnoticed.

Prompt-injection detection flags and does not block. A legitimate question could contain "ignore", and refusing it would be worse for a demo tool than passing a harmless flagged one to the system prompt, which is the layer that actually stops a followed instruction. I once reproduced the flag going quiet on repeat attempts, because `get_cached` rebuilt the result with `flagged_injection=False`. The flag belongs to the question, so it is recomputed on every path and never cached.

## Docker

Volumes bind-mount the repo over `/app` instead of baking in corpus, Chroma and the SQLite files. All three are locally built, and a bind mount makes a code change or re-ingest visible on restart without a rebuild. Mounting single files fails oddly: Docker creates an empty directory where the file should be and `sqlite3.connect()` then breaks.

The images were 9.73 GB, and 3.4 GB of that was CUDA. `torch` is an unpinned transitive dependency and pip on Linux picks the CUDA build, for a stack with no GPU. I measured inside the built image (`du -sh site-packages/*`) before believing it, then installed CPU-only torch from PyTorch's own index ahead of the requirements. The API image is now 2.73 GB after also splitting dependencies. The two Streamlit images are 803 MB each, because neither imports `src.rag` or `src.store` and neither needs torch, Chroma or the model SDKs. I confirmed that with `find_spec` returning `None` for the excluded packages. The embedding models are baked into the API image so a container starts without reaching HuggingFace, and `HF_HOME` points outside `/app` so the bind mount cannot shadow it.

A first `docker compose up -d` failed on port 8501 because a host Streamlit I had started for a screenshot was still bound to it. That was not a compose bug. Verifying app code locally and verifying its packaging are different jobs that collide on ports if run back to back.

## The agent experiment

`src/agent.py` was built to test one diagnosed failure, not "agents are better". Two golden items refused because a two-document comparison embeds as one query and under-retrieves the smaller document. The agent adds one capability: after retrieval, ask the model whether a named document is missing and, if so, issue one more targeted retrieval, capped at two requeries.

On the 10 multi-document items it fixed 0 refusals and introduced 0, at 2.76x the cost and 1.77x the latency. Both target items triggered the full two requeries and the missing document's chunks were in the merged context, so the mechanism worked. The final generation step still refused. A same-question re-run flipped one of them to a correct cited answer with identical code, which put the variance in the last step: whether the model commits to an answer from a larger, noisier context. I did not re-run until the numbers looked better. The negative result is reported as measured.

## Things that fought me

pip's resolver on `ragas`, `deepeval` and the rest of the requirements as one install ran for over an hour without finishing. It was bandwidth-bound, about 40 KB/s to PyPI, not a deadlock. Staging the install (light dependencies first, then Chroma and sentence-transformers) did not speed it up but made partial progress recoverable. `deepeval==2.5.6` pinned `pytest<8`, which conflicted with this project's pytest, so I moved it to a newer release with a lighter dependency list. I have since dropped RAGAS, but its import trouble stays on record: `ragas` unconditionally imported a `langchain_community` module that release 0.4 had removed, and the fix was pinning `langchain-community==0.3.31`, not chasing ragas versions. Its Anthropic adapter also set both `temperature` and `top_p`, which Claude rejects, and setting `top_p=None` is rejected too, so I deleted the key from the constructed LLM's `model_args`.

`chroma-hnswlib` has no prebuilt Windows wheel for this setup and needs Microsoft's C++ Build Tools. After that, torch itself would not load because Windows Smart App Control (in evaluation mode) blocked `torch_global_deps.dll` with WinError 4551. Smart App Control is a real security boundary, so I left it blocked until I turned it off myself in Windows Security and did not route around it.

chromadb 0.5.23 pulls `googleapis-common-protos`, which floors `protobuf>=6.33.5`, while Streamlit 1.40.2 caps `protobuf<6`. No single pin resolves a floor against a ceiling. A newer Streamlit relaxed the cap but wanted a newer `starlette`, which pushed FastAPI up too. Three packages, one graph, and I ran `pip check` after every bump until it came back clean.

chromadb's telemetry client raised on every call. Its `Posthog.capture()` uses the old three-argument signature, chromadb floors `posthog>=2.4.0` with no ceiling, and pip resolved a 7.x that replaced it with a single event object. `anonymized_telemetry=False` did not help, because `capture()` calls `posthog.capture()` regardless. Pinning `posthog==3.7.0` was the fix, and I kept the flag as well, since a local research tool has no reason to phone home.

One was my own doing: `obslog.py`'s `CREATE TABLE IF NOT EXISTS` is a no-op against a table that already exists, so a log created before two columns were added crashed the first insert. I wrote a real migration (`PRAGMA table_info` diffed against the expected columns) instead of deleting the file, since anyone upgrading with a kept log would hit the same crash.
